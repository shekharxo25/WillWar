## The studies

Researchers at the University of Plymouth asked people to rate a craving, play a block puzzle for three minutes, and rate it again.

In a 2014 lab study, people who played had weaker and less vivid cravings afterwards than people who waited through a loading screen. In a 2015 study, participants carried the puzzle on their phones for a week and played whenever a craving struck. Across food, drink, smoking and activities including sex, craving strength fell by roughly a fifth on average.

## Why it might work

Cravings often run on mental imagery: you picture the thing, and the picture pulls. A puzzle that keeps your visual attention busy competes for the same limited space, so the picture fades. That's the leading explanation, called elaborated intrusion theory, though it is still being tested.

## What to keep in mind

These were modest studies with self-reported ratings, and the effect is a short-term dip. A fifth weaker is not gone. But a fifth weaker at the right moment can be enough to let the urge pass.
